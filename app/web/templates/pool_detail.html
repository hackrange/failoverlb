{# ---- help ---- #}


  

    
Your backends sit on a port somewhere.  Anything that can reach that port
       can talk to them, and as far as the app can tell it looks the same as
       nginx.  A pool token is how the backend tells the difference.

  

  

    
How it works

    
nginx adds a header holding a shared secret to every request it proxies to
       this pool.  The backend checks it and returns 403 if it is missing or
       wrong.  A scanner that finds your app server on port 8080 gets nothing.

    
The header is set, not added, so a client sending that header itself has
       it thrown away and replaced.  You cannot forge your way in from outside.
       We also hide the header on the way back, in case the backend echoes it.

  

  

    
What it is not

    
It is a bearer secret.  Anybody holding it can pretend to be the fleet, so
       it belongs behind a firewall rather than instead of one.  If the hop to
       your backend crosses a network you do not trust, run that hop over TLS as
       well, or the token is readable to anybody watching.

  

  

    
Rotating without an outage

    
You cannot change both ends at the same instant, and nginx can only send
       one value, so it takes two steps.

    
1. Rotate.  A new token is generated but nginx keeps
       sending the old one.  Nothing changes on the wire.  Teach your backends
       to accept either.

    
2. Activate.  nginx starts sending the new one, which
       your backends already take.  Apply the config, watch traffic, then delete
       the old one from your backends.

    
Doing it in one step means every request in the gap gets a 403.

  

  

    
Health checks

    
The manager sends the token on its health checks too.  Without that, the
       moment your backend started enforcing it every check would come back 403,
       the pool would be marked down, and it would be pulled out of service
       while being perfectly healthy.

  

  

    
Turning it off

    
Turn it off here and apply the config first, then stop checking
       for it on your backends.  The other order locks the fleet out.

  






  
How nginx decides which backend gets each request.

  

    
What is available

    
    
      
  • Round robin takes turns.  Right when your backends are much the same.
    • 
      
  • Least connections picks whoever has fewest open.  Better when requests
          take very different amounts of time, because a slow backend piles up connections and
          naturally stops being chosen.
    • 
      
  • Pin by client address sends a given IP to the same backend.  Careful:
          a whole office behind one NAT address all lands on one server.
    • 
      
  • Pin by a value is the same idea on anything you name, usually a cookie.
          Nearly always better than pinning on the address.
    • 
      
  • Random two, least connections picks two at random and takes the quieter.
          Good on big pools, where plain least connections makes everything pile onto whichever
          server just freed up.
    • 
    

  

  

    
Not here

    
least_time is paid only.  Least connections tracks it closely in practice, and the
       adaptive weighting on the last tab gets you the rest of the way.

  






  

    
Changes what happens to everybody else when one backend goes away.

  

  

    
Why it matters

    
Without it, removing one backend from a pool of four reshuffles roughly everybody, because
       the hash is taken modulo the number of servers and that number just changed.  Every user
       lands somewhere new and, if you are relying on stickiness, every session breaks at once.

With it, only the users who were on the server that went away move.  Everybody else stays
exactly where they were.

  






  

    
How big a share of the traffic this backend takes.  Higher gets more.

  

  

    
Which way round it goes

    
This is a share, not a priority, and it is the opposite of the ranking most
       people expect.  A 10 is not first in line ahead of a 1.  A 10 takes ten times
       as many requests as a 1, and both are used at the same time.

    
Three backends on 1, 1 and 1 is an even split, a third each.  Change one to 2
       and it takes half of everything while the other two take a quarter each, because
       the shares are 2 out of 4, 1 out of 4 and 1 out of 4.

  

  

    
What it is for

    
Backends that are not the same size.  A box with twice the cores can carry twice
       the traffic, so give it 2 while the others stay on 1 rather than sending an even
       split to machines that cannot take it evenly.

    
Leave everything on 1 when the machines match, which is the normal case.

  

  

    
Not a way to take a backend out

    
There is no weight that means "do not use this".  Even a 1 next to a 100 still
       takes one request in a hundred.  To stop traffic reaching a backend, drain it or
       turn it off, both on this page.

  






  

    
The most requests nginx will have in flight to this backend at once.  Past that
       it queues rather than piling more on.

  

  

    
Zero means no limit

    
Which is the default, and is right until you know the number.  A limit invented
       out of nowhere turns a backend that was coping into one that queues.

  

  

    
When to set it

    
When the backend falls over under load rather than slowing down.  A database with
       a connection pool of 50 behind an app that opens one per request has a real limit,
       and it is better for nginx to hold requests than for the backend to start
       refusing them.

    
Set it to what the backend can actually take, not to what you hope it can.  The
       number belongs to the backend, not to this page.

  

  

    
What a visitor sees

    
Nothing, while the queue drains fast enough.  If it does not, they wait, and then
       they time out, so this trades a fast error for a slow success.  Watch the response
       column after changing it.

  






  

    
One question that settles three fiddly things, because getting any of them wrong
       looks like the proxy working.

  

  

    
Servers running my app

    
The normal case.  Several boxes behind one name, all serving the site your visitor
       asked for.  They get the visitor's name in the Host header, so they serve the right
       site and build links that point back at you.  Their redirects already point at your
       site, so nothing needs rewriting.

  

  

    
Another website

    
You are putting your name in front of something that already lives on its own name.
       It only answers to that name, so that is what goes in the Host header, and it is what
       we check its certificate against.

    
Its redirects point at itself.  Left alone, the first redirect walks your visitor off
       your site and onto the backend's address, and because that address works, it reads as
       fine until somebody looks at the address bar.  So those get pointed back at you.

  

  

    
What goes wrong if you pick the wrong one

    
Nothing that says "wrong Host header".  You get the backend's default site, or a 404,
       or a login page that redirects somewhere strange.  All three look like a problem with
       the backend, which is where the next hour goes.

  






  

    
Whether nginx speaks http or https on the hop from here to your backends.

  

  

    
When you need it

    
When the backend only listens for TLS, which a member on port 443 almost always does, and
       when the hop crosses anything you would not want somebody reading.  Inside a rack you
       control, plain http is fine and cheaper.

  

  

    
How it goes wrong without this

    
Sending plain http at a TLS port does not fail in a way that tells you anything.  The
       backend answers 400 Bad Request and nothing in the message mentions the scheme, so it
       looks like the backend is broken rather than like it is being spoken to in the wrong
       language.

  

  

    
The name we send

    
TLS needs a name, not an address, so nginx has to be told which one to ask for.  Its own
       default is the upstream block's name, and no backend has a certificate for
       your_pool_name, so we always send something real: what you type in the box,
       or the first member that is a hostname.

    
If every member is a bare IP there is no name to send, so we turn the whole thing off
       rather than put a made up one on the wire.  Certificates for bare addresses are rare and
       guessing at one would be wrong more often than right.

  

  

    
Checking the certificate

    
Turning this on encrypts the hop.  It does not on its own check who is on the other end of
       it.  For that, turn on proxy_ssl_verify in the site's settings, which is where
       the trusted certificate and the depth live too.

  

  

    
Health checks are separate

    
This setting covers real traffic.  The active health check has its own type on the Health
       tab, so a pool talking https should usually be checked with HTTPS as well.  Leaving that on
       HTTP means the check talks the wrong language to the same port that traffic works fine
       on.

  






  

    
How many idle connections to each backend nginx holds open for reuse.

  

  

    
Worth having

    
Without it every single request opens a fresh TCP connection, and over TLS pays for a
       full handshake too.  On a busy site that is a lot of work for nothing.

  

  

    
The catch

    
It only does anything if the proxy is speaking HTTP/1.1 and the Connection header is
       cleared.  The manager writes both of those for you, so setting a number here is enough.

  






  

    
A floor on how far the health checker is allowed to take the pool down.

  

  

    
What it protects against

    
If applying the failures would leave fewer than this many backends up, the manager leaves
       them all in instead and logs loudly.

A pool serving errors is bad.  A pool serving nothing at all, because a switch rebooted and
every check failed in the same second, is worse.  That failure mode is avoidable so we avoid it.

  






  
Two different things watch your backends.

  

    
nginx does passive checks

    
It notices when a real request fails and stops using that backend for a while.  That is
       the Fails and Timeout columns on the backends tab.  It is all the free nginx has on its
       own, and the catch is that a user had to get an error first.

  

  

    
The manager does active checks

    
It reaches out on a schedule whether or not anybody is browsing.  When a backend fails
       enough times in a row the upstream is rewritten with that server marked down and nginx
       reloads.  So it is out of rotation before a user finds it.

Every node checks separately and the results are kept apart, so a backend one load balancer can
reach and another cannot shows up as exactly that, which is usually the whole answer.

  

  

    
Picking the numbers

    
Down after 3 at a 5 second interval means a dead backend is out within about 15 seconds.
       Lower is twitchier and reacts to a single dropped packet.  Higher is calmer and slower.

  






  

    
What to put in the Host header when checking.

  

  

    
When you need it

    
If the backend serves several sites by name, a request with no Host, or the wrong one,
       gets the default site or a 404.  The check then reports the backend as down when it is
       perfectly healthy, and you spend an hour looking at the wrong thing.

  






  

    
Keeps a user on the same backend, which apps holding session state in local memory need.

  

  

    
How this is done here

    
The paid nginx has a sticky cookie feature.  The free one does not, but it can hash on
       anything, so the manager writes the cookie half: a map that reads a route cookie, a header
       that sets it when the client has none, and an upstream hashing on it with consistent
       turned on.

Same result, put together from parts that are in the free build.

  

  

    
Worth saying

    
The real fix is not needing it.  Put sessions in redis or the database and any backend can
       serve any user, so you can take a server out whenever you like.  Sticky sessions are a
       workaround, and they come with a cost: when a backend dies, everybody on it is logged out.

  






  

    
Eases traffic back onto a backend that just came back, rather than all at once.

  

  

    
Why

    
A JVM app that just restarted has a cold JIT, an empty connection pool and no warm cache.
       Give it a third of your traffic the instant it passes one health check and it can fall
       straight back over.  Then it recovers, gets slammed again, and flaps.  That is worse than
       it having stayed down.

  

  

    
How it works here

    
The manager ramps the weight in a handful of steps across your window.  Not as smooth as
       doing it inside nginx, since each step is a config reload, but the point is simply to not
       hit a cold server with everything at once, and steps do that fine.

  






  

    
Leans traffic toward whichever backends are answering fastest.

  

  

    
Where the numbers come from

    
The health checker is already timing every check, so it knows which backends are slow.
       This turns that into weights.

  

  

    
The rails on it

    
Weights move one step at a time between a floor and a ceiling, so one slow check cannot
       pull a server out of rotation.  And if everything is slow, nothing changes, because that
       means the problem is downstream and shuffling weights would just pile load onto whichever
       box is least broken.

  

  

    
Honest comparison

    
The paid least_time reacts per request.  This reacts on a cycle, so minutes not
       milliseconds.  Good for backends that are consistently different speeds, like mixed
       hardware.  For traffic that spikes in seconds, plain least connections reacts faster and
       you should just use that.
