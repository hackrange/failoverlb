Adding up stars is the crudest reading of this table there is. It says a request queue and a support contract weigh the same, and they do not. It says packaged video streaming is worth exactly one row, when for the people who need it that row decides the whole question on its own. The total is published because it was asked for, in the order the numbers actually fall. A comparison that only prints its totals when they flatter the author is not a comparison.

One thing worth knowing about how this total can be moved. Backup and restore used to be a single row and is now three, because taking a copy, what is in the copy and what you can do with it later are genuinely different jobs. That change alone moved us up seven stars and moved Kemp up six. Splitting a category is the oldest way to tilt a table like this, and the person doing the splitting was us, so it is said here rather than left for somebody to notice. It could as easily have been six rows. It is three because backing up should not outweigh load balancing on a page about load balancers.

What we could not rebuild

Being straight about the gaps matters more than a tidy list.

  • HLS, DASH and f4f streaming. These are compiled into the nginx binary and there is no way to add them from out here. If you need packaged video streaming that is a real reason to buy the paid version.
  • NTLM connection pinning. It keeps a connection pinned through a Windows authentication handshake, and that cannot be faked from outside the process. If you proxy to something needing NTLM, this one will actually bite you.
  • MQTT preread and filter. Stream level MQTT parsing. No way around it.
  • The App Protect WAF. A separate paid product, and this line used to say we were not going to pretend a few regular expressions were a web application firewall. That is no longer where we are: the installer builds ModSecurity, the OWASP rules are installed and kept current across the fleet, and a site can be put in front of them from its own tab. App Protect is still the better product. The part that makes a WAF survivable, learning what your own application does and turning that into exclusions, is now built. See the WAF row above.
  • In flight request counts on the dashboard. Our numbers come from the access log, and a request that has not finished has not been logged yet.

A note on reloads

Most of what is above works by rewriting config and reloading, so it is worth being comfortable with what that does. On reload, the nginx master reads the new config and starts new workers with it. The old workers stop taking new connections but keep serving the requests they already have until those finish, then they exit. No connection gets cut and nobody sees an error. Two things to know. A reload costs a little CPU and briefly runs two sets of workers, so memory goes up for a moment. And if you have very long lived connections like WebSockets, the old workers stick around until those close. The manager already debounces reloads, so twenty backends flapping at once turns into one reload rather than twenty.
